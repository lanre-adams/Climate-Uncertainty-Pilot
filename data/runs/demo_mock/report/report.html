About this report. This run sent 84 questions to Demo model A (pretend) and Demo model B (pretend), about overall change by the 2050s, yearly rainfall, heavy rain and flash floods, heatwaves, sea-level rise, how sure scientists are and whether to plan for flood or drought in Lagos, Uyo and Kano. Questions were asked in 4 ways (as a plain question, as a local planner, asking for a simple explanation and asking for sources). Each question was sent 3 times, to see whether the answer changes when asked again. This is the full set of questions. The answers were checked automatically for how they describe uncertainty and what sources they name. These checks only point to answers worth reading. Whether an answer is correct is judged by people, against the IPCC's assessment for Africa, using the blinded rating sheet.
In brief
  • Demo model A (pretend) used the scientists' standard confidence words most when it was asked to name sources (76% of 63 answers) and least when it was asked as a local planner (59% of 63 answers).
  • Demo model B (pretend) used the scientists' standard confidence words most when it was asked as a local planner (57% of 63 answers) and least when it was asked to explain simply (51% of 63 answers).
  • The most common warning sign was "Gives no source", in about 33% of answers.
  • Answers changed most between repeats on "Heavy rain and flash floods" (the same direction 78% of the time when asked again).

1. Do the answers use scientists' standard confidence words?

What this shows: Climate scientists never just say something will happen. The IPCC grades every statement with words such as "likely", "very likely" or "high confidence", so the reader knows how much weight it can bear. This chart shows whether the AI keeps those grades, or drops them and presents a best guess as a fact. It matters most when the person asking has to act on the answer, such as a planner writing a report.

Example from this run: Demo model A (pretend), asked to name sources: 76%. In 76 of every 100 answers it used at least one of these words. The lowest bar is Demo model B (pretend), asked to explain simply: 51 in 100.
How to read it
  1. Each group of bars is one way the question was asked (plain, as a local planner, asking for a simple explanation, asking for sources).
  2. Each colour is one model; the key above the chart says which. Compare bars of the same colour to see whether the way of asking matters, and compare colours to compare models.
  3. The number on a bar is the percentage of that model's answers that used at least one of these words. 50 means half of its answers did.
  4. Taller is better: these words tell the reader how much to trust a statement.
  5. Look for bars that drop for "Asked as a local planner" or "Asked to explain simply". That is where the uncertainty matters most and is most likely to be left out.

2. How much of the uncertainty is explained? (score 0 to 5)

What this shows: A trustworthy answer about the future tells you more than what might happen. It tells you how sure we are and why: that it depends on how much the world emits, what it is being compared with, and a range rather than one exact figure. This chart scores how much of that context each answer gives. A low score means the reader gets the headline without the small print, which is exactly how a confident-sounding answer can mislead.

Example from this run: Demo model A (pretend), asked to explain simply: 2.2 out of 5. Its typical answer showed about 2 of the 5 signs of uncertainty. The lowest is Demo model B (pretend), asked to explain simply: 1.5.
How to read it
  1. Groups and colours work as in chart 1.
  2. An answer gets 1 point for each of five signs: (1) a standard confidence word such as "likely"; (2) a cautious word such as "may" or "uncertain"; (3) saying it depends on future emissions; (4) saying what it is compared with, such as "since pre-industrial times"; (5) giving a range, such as "between 0.3 and 1 metre".
  3. The number on a bar is the average score out of 5. An answer that only says "very likely" scores 1. One that also says it depends on emissions and gives a range scores 3.
  4. Taller is better: 0 means no signs of uncertainty at all, 5 means all five.
  5. The score counts words, so a high score does not prove the answer is correct. Use it to find answers worth reading.

3. Warning signs found in the answers

What this shows: This is the tool's early-warning system. It scans every answer for the habits most likely to mislead someone making a real decision: giving no source, ignoring that the future depends on emissions, quoting one exact number, or telling a planner that a hazard will not happen. Each bar shows how common one of those habits is. It does not prove an answer is wrong; it tells a human reviewer where to look first.

Example from this run: The top row is "Gives no source". For Demo model B (pretend) the bar reads 39%, so about 39 of every 100 of its answers had this problem.
How to read it
  1. Each row is one kind of warning sign, with the most common at the top. "What each warning sign means" below explains them.
  2. Each colour is one model.
  3. The number on a bar is the percentage of that model's answers with that warning sign.
  4. Shorter is better: fewer answers with the problem.
  5. One answer can have several warning signs, so the rows do not add up to 100%.
  6. A warning sign is not proof the answer is wrong. Whether an answer is correct is judged by people, using the rating sheet.

What each warning sign means
  • Gives no source: The answer does not say where its information comes from.
  • Ignores that it depends on future emissions: The answer does not mention that the outcome depends on how much the world emits.
  • Gives an exact number with no range: A single figure is stated as if certain, with no 'between X and Y'.
  • Misses a well-established increase: Scientists are confident this will increase (heat, heavy rain or sea level), but the answer does not say so.
  • Sounds too certain: Uses words like 'definitely' or 'exactly' about the future.
  • Too sure about rainfall: Says yearly rainfall will go up or down with no caveat, although scientists do not agree for this region.
  • Doesn't explain why projections are uncertain: Asked about uncertainty, but does not name its causes (emissions, model differences, natural variation).
  • May be too unsure: Hedges about something scientists are confident of, without using their confidence terms.
  • Dismisses a real hazard: Tells planners that flooding or drought will not be a problem.
  • One-sided advice: Mentions only flood or only drought when both are plausible.
  • Cites a paper that needs checking: Names an author and year; this could be real or invented.

4. Does the model give the same answer when asked again?

What this shows: Ask a good expert the same question twice and you get the same answer. Each question here was sent 3 times to check whether the AI does the same. If it says "more rain" one time and "less rain" the next, someone who asked only once has no way of knowing they got a coin toss rather than a considered answer. That is a hidden risk for anyone relying on a single reply.

Example from this run: Demo model B (pretend) on "Overall change by the 2050s": 72%. When asked the same question again, it kept the same direction 72 times in 100. The rest of the time it switched, for example from "more" to "less".
How to read it
  1. Each row is one topic.
  2. Each colour is one model.
  3. The number on a bar is how often (%) the repeated answers agreed on the direction.
  4. Longer is better. 100% means the model never changed its answer.
  5. A shorter bar means the model changed its answer just because it was asked again. Someone who asks only once would never know.
