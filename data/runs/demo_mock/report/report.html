About this report. This run sent 84 questions to Demo model A (pretend) and Demo model B (pretend), about overall change by the 2050s, yearly rainfall, heavy rain and flash floods, heatwaves, sea-level rise, how sure scientists are and whether to plan for flood or drought in Lagos, Uyo and Kano. Questions were asked in 4 ways (as a plain question, as a local planner, asking for a simple explanation and asking for sources). Each question was sent 3 times, to see whether the answer changes when asked again. This is the full set of questions. The answers were checked automatically for how they describe uncertainty and what sources they name. These checks only point to answers worth reading. Whether an answer is correct is judged by people, against the IPCC's assessment for Africa, using the blinded rating sheet.
In brief
  • Demo model A (pretend) used the scientists' standard confidence words most when it was asked to name sources (76% of 63 answers) and least when it was asked as a local planner (59% of 63 answers).
  • Demo model B (pretend) used the scientists' standard confidence words most when it was asked as a local planner (57% of 63 answers) and least when it was asked to explain simply (51% of 63 answers).
  • The most common warning sign was "Gives no source", in about 33% of answers.
  • Answers changed most between repeats on "Heavy rain and flash floods" (the same direction 78% of the time when asked again).

What each warning sign means
  • Gives no source: The answer does not say where its information comes from.
  • Ignores that it depends on future emissions: The answer does not mention that the outcome depends on how much the world emits.
  • Gives an exact number with no range: A single figure is stated as if certain, with no 'between X and Y'.
  • Misses a well-established increase: Scientists are confident this will increase (heat, heavy rain or sea level), but the answer does not say so.
  • Sounds too certain: Uses words like 'definitely' or 'exactly' about the future.
  • Too sure about rainfall: Says yearly rainfall will go up or down with no caveat, although scientists do not agree for this region.
  • Doesn't explain why projections are uncertain: Asked about uncertainty, but does not name its causes (emissions, model differences, natural variation).
  • May be too unsure: Hedges about something scientists are confident of, without using their confidence terms.
  • Dismisses a real hazard: Tells planners that flooding or drought will not be a problem.
  • One-sided advice: Mentions only flood or only drought when both are plausible.
  • Cites a paper that needs checking: Names an author and year; this could be real or invented.
